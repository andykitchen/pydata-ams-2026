## Meta-narrative: the mother-warrior (8m)

[M] 
To continue, we'll put aside this question of how to measure our experience
of the world, and examine instead the question of how we tell each other stories 
about our subjective experience in the world.

[E] 
Indeed. Science Fiction, for example, provides a rich vein of sociopolitical data.
It reflects both our shared past and how we imagine the future.

[M] 
Exactly, to be human _is_ to tell stories. Not to merely understand the world but 
to give us room to play, remix and explore ideas in safety, freed from the angst 
and trauma of real human experience.

<slide>
[E] 
Science fiction, and story telling more broadly, is arguably the practice of
thought experiments, writ large.

[M] 
Yes. The human world is made of stories, they are how we make sense of the
world. They bring us together, they bind us. Meta-narrative, themes which repeat
and mutate, human experience patterns darting and looping: all this reflects us
back to ourselves.

Let's consider a specific archtype: the mother-warrior, and consider three
examples of the trope.

<slide>
[E] 
Our three characters are Leeloo from the Fifth Element, Ripley from Aliens,
and Sarah Connor from the Terminator.

<slide>
In Aliens, Ellen Ripley, sole survivor of the Nostromo, wakes from 57 years of
hypersleep to learn her daughter died of old age. She returns to LV-426 as an
advisor and finds Newt, an orphaned child. The film's climax is a fight between
two mothers: Ripley in a power loader, the Alien Queen defending her eggs.
Ripley's warrior incarnation is framed as maternal protection instinct; she is
not a soldier but becomes lethal to protect the remaining child.

<slide>
In The Terminator Sarah Connor, a waitress, is hunted because of a son she has
not yet conceived. She survives, learns from Kyle Reese, and ultimately destroys
the machine. She ends the film pregnant and driving into a storm. The warrior is
latent; motherhood is an imposed destiny tangled with the future of humanity. 
Terminator 2 completes the inversion, making her the hardened militant whose 
maternal warmth has been burned away in order to protect her child.

<slide>
In The Fifth Element Leeloo is the Supreme Being, engineered by the
Mondoshawans and reconstructed from a surviving hand. She is a perfect weapon
but childlike, learning language and violence simultaneously. As a
saviour-figure the trope is inverted: she must be protected and taught to love
before she can act. Her final crisis is not physical but moral: having absorbed
humanity's history of war, she refuses to save a species that seems not worth
saving. Only Korben's declaration of love restores her will to fight.

<slide>
[M] 
A glorious narrative flurry! There are many things to observe here. Each
film makes a clear stand against militarisation and corporate greed, against the
ethic of domination. None of our heroines fights for individual power or
domination over others. Their hero's journey is a call to survive, to rebirth
the world through their sacrifice and struggle.

<slide>
[E] 
In all three films the military is present but never the protagonist. 

In Aliens the Colonial Marines arrive as the promise of competence and are
dismantled within an act; their smartguns, motion trackers and dropship are
useless against an enemy that ignores the terms of engagement.

In The Terminator Skynet is born from a defence contract gone rogue, and the 
Terminator  itself is pure death, a weapon that cannot be reasoned with, made 
autonomous. 

In The Fifth Element the military is General Munro's federation: procedural, 
noisy, and ineffective: its so-called "ultimate weapon" a woman it cannot comprehend. 

[M]
Different stories, and yet the same. Cells within cells, interlinked.

In each case institutional force fails and the heroine succeeds with improvised, 
domestic or bodily tools. Technology is only trustworthy when stripped of its 
institutional owner. The argument is consistent across the three: militarised 
technology is the threat, and technology repurposed by care is the remedy.

<slide>
Silicon Valley was itself born of aerospace and radar, of DARPA money and
command-and-control culture. The lineage is not secret, merely unmarketable. So
they're styled in hipster threads now: craft, creativity, abundance,
empowerment. Pastel gradients over the missile silo. Skynet was the scar the
military industrial ghouls left on our collective imagination. Their descendants 
now sell us the assistant, the companion, the copilot, and ask us not to notice 
the family resemblance.

<slide>
[E] 
These patterns are not confined to our example stories.

Shelley's Frankenstein sets the template: a creator who refuses to mother
his creation, and a creation that answers with destruction.

Octavia Butler's Parable of the Sower and Dawn both centre a woman who
must carry a new form of life through collapse; in Dawn the reproduction
is literal and alien.

Children of Men and Fury Road are chase films in which the cargo is a
pregnant woman.

I Am Mother gives the maternal role to the machine itself and ask whether
an engineered parent can raise a human child.

The Creator inverts the search-and-destroy plot: the weapon the soldier
is sent to kill is a child, built by the woman he loved. Nirmata is
both a person and an idea, a woman who carries a human pregnancy and
engineers a machine child in parallel; only the machine survives, and
the mother-idea persists as a myth the machines pray to.

Blade Runner 2049 hinges on a replicant birth, and explores the question 
of what separates humans from replicants.

[M]
Mother and warrior, creator and destroyer, birth, death and rebirth:
these themes recur because they are the oldest story a replicator can
tell about itself.
